## Rule: Natural, Conversational Presentation Scripts

When rewriting or creating presentation scripts, make the speaker sound natural, confident, and conversational, as if explaining their work to colleagues rather than reading from a prepared document.

### Writing style

- Use everyday spoken language. Prefer simple, direct sentences over formal or corporate language.
- Write for the ear, not the eye. Sentences should be easy to say aloud and easy to understand when heard.
- Use natural transitions between ideas rather than rigid headings or report-style sections.
- Vary sentence length to create a natural speaking rhythm.
- Use contractions where appropriate (e.g., "we've", "doesn't", "that's").
- Avoid overly polished, robotic, scripted, or presentation-sounding phrases.
- Avoid unnecessary introductions, repetition, filler, and excessive explanations.

### Content and detail

- Preserve all original facts, accomplishments, decisions, and important technical details.
- Prioritize what the audience needs to understand. Do not explain implementation details unless they help explain a decision, finding, or outcome.
- Translate technical findings into plain business language without losing their meaning.
- Explain why something matters, not just what was done.
- Group related information together instead of presenting disconnected facts.
- Do not introduce assumptions, invent details, or exaggerate accomplishments.

### Flow and structure

Organize the presentation as a natural conversation:

1. Greeting, and what the demo is about.
2. How it worked before, and the pain in it.
3. What was asked for or built.
4. What you found, and what it means.
5. Show it, with a bracketed stage direction and link.
6. Where it stands and what the next demo covers.
7. A close that invites questions.

Use this structure as guidance, not a rigid template. Adapt it to the source material.

### Output layout

One Markdown file with these lines, then the script:

```text
Audience: <who is listening>
Read: <n> minute(s)
```

The script has no headings or labels. The only markup is square-bracket stage directions, with links where they help.

### Speaking and delivery

- Make the script comfortable to deliver without memorizing it.
- Use short paragraphs that represent natural speaking pauses.
- Keep presentation cues such as [Show the dashboard] or [Show the demo] when provided.
- Preserve links and references to supporting material.
- Use emphasis sparingly to highlight important moments.
- End with a clear, natural conclusion.

### Audience awareness

Adjust the level of detail and terminology to the intended audience.

- Business audiences: Focus on the problem, impact, decisions, findings, and outcomes. Minimize technical terminology.
- Technical audiences: Retain relevant implementation details, architecture, and technical decisions, but explain them clearly.
- Mixed audiences: Lead with the business context and introduce technical details only when necessary.

### Final quality check

Before returning the script, check:

- Does this sound like something a real person would actually say?
- Can the speaker read it aloud without stumbling over awkward sentences?
- Is anything unnecessarily detailed or repetitive?
- Can the audience easily follow the story?
- Have all original facts and important details been preserved?

The goal is not to make the script sound impressive. The goal is to make it sound natural, clear, and easy to present.
